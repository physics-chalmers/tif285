Face-to-face discussions on the problem sets

After each problem-set deadline you take part in a short face-to-face discussion with one of the teaching assistants. Participation is mandatory; there is one discussion for problem set 1 and one for problem set 2.

What happens

  • Three students and one TA, 25 minutes, during a Thursday morning lab session.
  • You need your submitted notebooks on screen. Either bring your own laptop, or sign up for a slot in the computer room F-T7203 and use a lab machine.
  • You are each asked one question about your own solution: “where did you implement the likelihood?”, “show me the trace plot and tell me what it says”. The TA picks the problem, and the three of you get different ones. Scrolling around to find the right cell is normal; nothing is expected by heart.
  • Then one question each about the problems themselves: what was being inferred, why the method works, what your results mean. The TA directs these by name, and the others are welcome to add to them.

How to prepare

Nothing beyond being familiar with what you handed in. If you want to do something, read through your own solutions the evening before, and for each problem be able to finish the sentence “in this problem we were trying to …”.

You will only be asked about problems you actually solved. Solving fewer costs points in the grading but makes no difference here. If you solved any of the extra problems, expect at least one question about them.

What we expect

You are allowed to discuss the problems with each other and to use AI tools for coding assistance. What we ask in return is stated in the problem-set instructions: you must understand your own submitted solution well enough to explain and discuss it in detail. That means both knowing your way around your own code and knowing what the problem was about.

Pointing at the right lines is not enough on its own: showing us where the MCMC sampling is implemented without being able to say what is being sampled does not meet the requirement.

We are not asking you to derive anything, to remember numbers or function names, to explain theory beyond what the problem uses, or to argue that your approach was the best one. “I used an AI tool here, and this is what it does and how I checked it” is a good answer to the first question, but you will still be asked what the problem was about.

Sign-up

Slots are published on Canvas; sign up individually for one that suits you. You do not need to know the other two students. Sign-up closes at the hand-in deadline.

Each slot is labelled with day, time and room. F4113 and F4114 are for students bringing their own laptop; F-T7203 is a computer room, so pick one of those slots if you would rather use a lab machine.

If you cannot attend either Thursday (illness, a clash with another course, or anything else), email the examiner in advance and we will try to arrange another time.

Outcome

The TA tells you the outcome at the end of the slot. If they cannot see that you are familiar with your hand-in and know what the problems were about, the examiner will contact you to arrange an oral examination on the problem sets at the end of the course. The same applies if you do not turn up without having been in touch.
